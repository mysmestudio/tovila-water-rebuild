Designing an Industrial RO System: The Critical Inputs

An industrial reverse osmosis system is a thermodynamic mass-transfer separation unit, not a collection of off-the-shelf plumbing parts. Here is the rigorous engineering methodology required to establish design basis, flux rates, array staging, and hydraulic boundaries.

In industrial water engineering, the single greatest cause of plant failure is “template engineering” — copying the mechanical layout, vessel staging, and pump curves from a previous successful installation and applying them to a new site without recalculating mass balances against the new water chemistry.

A robust basis-of-design (BOD) must precede any hardware procurement. Below are the mandatory engineering inputs and procedural workflows every process engineer must establish before running membrane simulation projections (such as DuPont WAVE, Hydranautics IMSDesign, or TorayDS2).

1. Comprehensive Raw Water Chemistry & Anion-Cation Balance

A basic “drinking water test” reporting TDS and pH is completely insufficient for industrial membrane design. The process engineer requires a complete ionic profile reported in mg/L (or meq/L):

Cations

Calcium (Ca2+), Magnesium (Mg2+), Sodium (Na+), Potassium (K+), Strontium (Sr2+), Barium (Ba2+), Total & Dissolved Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), and Ammonium (NH4+).

Anions & Physicals

Chloride (Cl−), Sulfate (SO42−), Bicarbonate (HCO3−), Carbonate (CO32−), Nitrate (NO3−), Fluoride (F−), Reactive Silica (SiO2), pH, Temperature range, and SDI15.

The engineer must verify that the Cation-Anion Meq/L Balance closes within ±5%. A failure to close indicates missing ions (such as unanalyzed organic acids or unmeasured divalent metals) that will corrupt solubility index calculations.

2. Design Flux Selection Based on Raw Water Source

Permeate Flux (J) is the volumetric rate of water passing through a unit area of membrane surface, expressed in litres per square metre per hour (LMH) or gallons per square foot per day (GFD):

Empirical Flux Limits by Water Source

  • Wastewater Tertiary Effluent (MBR / UF feed): 10 – 14 LMH (6 – 8 GFD)
  • Surface Water (Lake, River with conventional pretreatment): 14 – 18 LMH (8 – 11 GFD)
  • Well / Groundwater (Borehole supply, low fouling): 19 – 25 LMH (11 – 15 GFD)
  • Seawater (Open intake): 12 – 15 LMH (7 – 9 GFD)
  • High Purity RO Permeate (Second-pass polishers): 25 – 34 LMH (15 – 20 GFD)

Designing for an aggressive flux rate reduces initial capital cost by requiring fewer membrane elements and pressure vessels. However, exceeding the empirical source boundary causes exponential concentration polarization, rapid irreversible fouling, and severe ΔP spikes.

3. Array Staging & Stage Flow Ratio

To achieve high recovery (e.g. 75% or 80%) on brackish feed water without exceeding element flow limits, membrane vessels are arranged in multi-stage arrays (e.g., 2:1 staging).

In a 2-stage system, the concentrate (reject) of Stage 1 becomes the feed for Stage 2. Because approximately half of the water has been extracted as permeate in Stage 1, the number of pressure vessels in Stage 2 must be halved (a 2:1 staging ratio) to maintain sufficient cross-flow velocity across the tail elements and sweep away rejected salts.

  • Lead Element Flux: Must not exceed manufacturer maximum flux (typically ≤ 30 LMH for brackish water).
  • Tail Element Concentrate Flow: Must not drop below minimum brine flow (typically ≥ 2.7 to 3.2 m3/h for standard 8040 elements) to prevent stagnant boundary layer nucleation.

4. Net Driving Pressure (NDP) Calculations

The booster pump must generate enough total dynamic head to overcome both the membrane hydraulic resistance and the osmotic pressure differential:

Net Driving Pressure Formula

NDP = Pfeed − (ΔParray / 2) − Ppermeate − Δπ

Where Δπ is the average osmotic pressure difference between the concentrate and permeate streams (governed by the Van ’t Hoff equation: π ≈ 1.12 × (T + 273) × ΣMi).

5. Temperature Envelope & Viscosity Variations

Water viscosity is inversely proportional to temperature. For every 1°C drop in water temperature, permeate production declines by approximately 2.5% to 3.0% at identical pump pressure.

Industrial systems in Ghana must be engineered for both the wet season minimum and dry season maximum raw water temperatures. Sizing the booster pump only for 30°C warm water will cause severe production shortfalls if feed water drops to 22°C during rainy periods.
