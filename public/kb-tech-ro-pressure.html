Why Does an RO System Lose Pressure?

In reverse osmosis plants, pressure is the prime mover that forces pure water through the semi-permeable membrane barrier. When pressure collapses or differential pressure spikes, here is how technicians diagnose the root cause sequentially.

When a commercial or industrial Reverse Osmosis (RO) skid triggers a low-pressure fault or suffers an unexplained drop in permeate flow, amateur operators often adjust the bypass needle valve or crank up pump speed. This is dangerous and can quickly destroy the membrane elements.

In water engineering, pressure loss is never random — it is an empirical symptom of hydraulic restriction, mechanical wear, or membrane surface resistance. Experienced technicians follow a disciplined sequential diagnostic workflow rather than guessing.

The Seven Primary Causes of RO Pressure Loss

  1. Clogged Pre-treatment Cartridges: High differential pressure (ΔP) across sediment or carbon filter housings starves the high-pressure booster pump intake of water, causing suction cavitation.
  2. Fouled or Scaled Membrane Elements: Inorganic mineral precipitation (calcium carbonate/sulfate) or organic biofilm increases hydraulic resistance, choking cross-flow velocity.
  3. Booster Pump Impeller Wear or Cavitation Damage: Erosion of stainless steel impellers or worn mechanical seals reducing pump head output.
  4. Low Feed Supply Pressure: Insufficient pressure from municipal mains or raw water transfer pumps failing to meet the minimum Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) required by the RO skid.
  5. Partially Closed, Throttled, or Seized Valves: A manual ball valve accidentally knocked off-position, or an inlet solenoid valve failing to open fully due to coil burnout or sediment jamming.
  6. Air Locks in High-Pressure Piping: Entrapped air bubbles in pump heads or membrane vessel manifolds causing erratic pressure oscillations and false low-pressure trips.
  7. Failed or Leaking Check Valves: Permeate back-pressure check valves leaking, allowing pressurized product water or concentrate to short-circuit.

The Sequential Gauge Diagnostic Protocol

Do not adjust control valves until you have recorded readings across every pressure gauge in sequence from the raw feed line to the concentrate outlet:

Stage 01

Feed Inlet Gauge

Measures raw water supply pressure upstream of the 5-micron pre-filter. Must meet skid minimum (typically 30–45 PSI / 2–3 bar).

Stage 02

Post-Cartridge Gauge

Measures pressure leaving the cartridge housing. Calculate ΔP = (Stage 1 − Stage 2). If ΔP > 15 PSI (1.0 bar), replace cartridges immediately.

Stage 03

Pump Discharge Gauge

Measures operating pressure delivered to membrane vessels. Verify reading against design pump curve and motor VFD frequency.

Understanding ΔP (Differential Pressure)

Differential pressure (ΔP) is the pressure drop across a membrane vessel or array:
ΔP = Feed Pressure − Concentrate (Reject) Pressure
A normal ΔP for a clean multi-element pressure vessel is typically 15 to 25 PSI (1.0 to 1.7 bar). If ΔP rises by 15% to 20% above baseline commissioning value, the feed spacers are fouling with silt, bio-slime, or mineral scale.

Step-by-Step Isolation Checklist

Follow this procedure when troubleshooting on-site:

  • Step 1: Check the Cartridge Filter ΔP. If the pressure drop across the 5-micron pre-filter exceeds 10–15 PSI, the booster pump is starved. Replace the cartridge element before testing further.
  • Step 2: Inspect the Low-Pressure Cut-off Switch. Most RO plants feature an automated low-pressure switch (typically set between 15 and 25 PSI) to protect the high-pressure pump from running dry. Check wiring and calibration.
  • Step 3: Listen for Pump Cavitation. A pump that sounds like it is pumping marbles or gravel is cavitating — vapor bubbles are imploding against the impeller. This confirms starvation from upstream restrictions.
  • Step 4: Check Vessel Inter-Stage Pressures. In a two-stage RO system (e.g. 2:1 vessel array), compare Stage 1 ΔP against Stage 2 ΔP. If Stage 1 ΔP is elevated, the cause is colloidal silt or biofouling. If Stage 2 ΔP is elevated, the cause is mineral scaling in the concentrate stream.
  • Step 5: Inspect Concentrate Needle Valve. Throttling valves can accumulate grit or antiscalant precipitates, causing false concentrate back-pressures or flow restrictions.

Preventative Best Practices for Plant Operators

Accurate daily operational logs are the technician’s most powerful diagnostic tool. Record feed pressure, post-filter pressure, pump discharge pressure, concentrate pressure, permeate flow, concentrate flow, and temperature at the exact same hour each day.

Normalizing your data (adjusting for temperature and feed TDS fluctuations) allows you to detect pressure drops and membrane fouling weeks before the skid trips a mechanical fault.
